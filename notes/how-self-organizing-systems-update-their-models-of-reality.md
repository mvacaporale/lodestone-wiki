# How self-organizing systems update their models of reality

**The same computational signature governs model updating from neurons to civilizations.** Across neuroscience, developmental psychology, complexity science, philosophy of science, anthropology, and contemplative traditions, a remarkably consistent pattern emerges: self-organizing systems update their generative models through precision-weighted prediction error — the regulated encounter between what a system expects and what actually happens. Whether the system is a traumatized brain, a stagnant organization, or industrial civilization running an obsolete growth model, pathological rigidity takes the same formal shape: excessive confidence in existing priors, insufficient sensitivity to incoming evidence, and a progressive divergence between the internal model and the actual structure of the environment. This divergence is what Karl Friston's Free Energy Principle identifies as rising free energy — the mathematical signature of a system losing its grip on reality. The practical implication is that model updating can be facilitated at every scale through the same fundamental levers: loosening rigid priors, amplifying prediction errors, ensuring sufficient energy for reorganization, and providing what every tradition calls, in its own language, a "holding environment."

---

## The universal architecture of model updating

The Free Energy Principle provides the formal backbone. Every self-organizing system that persists over time behaves as if it maintains a generative model — an internal representation of the causal structure of its environment — and minimizes the divergence between that model's predictions and incoming sensory evidence. This minimization can happen two ways: **perceptual inference** (revising the model to better accommodate reality) or **active inference** (acting on the world to make it match predictions). The balance between these two strategies is governed by **precision** — the confidence assigned to prediction errors versus prior beliefs. High precision on priors means the system trusts its existing model and resists revision. High precision on prediction errors means evidence drives rapid updating.

This architecture nests across scales through what Friston calls Markov blankets — statistical boundaries that separate a system's internal states from external states while mediating their interaction. Kirchhoff, Parr, and Friston demonstrated that Markov blankets self-assemble hierarchically: cells within tissues within organs within organisms within social groups within institutions within civilizations. At each level, the same free energy minimization dynamics operate, and the same precision-weighting mechanism determines whether the system updates its model or doubles down on failing predictions. Michael Levin's multi-scale competency architecture confirms this empirically: molecular networks, cells, tissues, and organisms each solve problems in their own action space, and each level deforms the energy landscape for levels above and below.

This formal machinery maps onto frameworks that predate it by decades. **Piaget's equilibration** — the cycle from equilibrium through disequilibrium to accommodation — is perceptual inference triggered by prediction error. **Argyris and Schön's double-loop learning** — questioning governing variables rather than merely correcting actions — is model structure updating rather than parameter adjustment. **Kuhn's paradigm shift** — the crisis-driven replacement of one scientific framework with another — is Bayesian model selection at the collective scale. **Holling's adaptive cycle** — the movement from rigid conservation through release to reorganization — is the thermodynamic envelope within which model updating occurs. The convergence is not metaphorical. These are different disciplinary windows onto the same underlying process.

## What blocks updating: the anatomy of rigidity

The most striking finding across disciplines is how consistently the same barriers to model updating appear at every scale. At the neural level, **pathologically precise priors** — deeply held predictions that suppress incoming evidence — underlie depression (overly confident negative self-models), PTSD (frozen threat models that override safety signals), addiction (rigid action-perception loops), and anxiety (chronic uncertainty about one's own agency). Robin Carhart-Harris and Friston's REBUS model frames these as conditions where the brain's "energy landscape" has deepened into narrow, steep valleys: the system is trapped in a local minimum, unable to explore alternative configurations.

Trauma creates particularly instructive rigidity. Bessel van der Kolk's research demonstrates that traumatic memories persist with little modification over time, stored somatically rather than narratively — "the body keeps the score." In predictive processing terms, Linson and Friston describe a "traumatized generative model" where top-down threat priors become decoupled from bottom-up sensory processing, making the organism react to internal cues rather than current reality. Peter Levine's Somatic Experiencing identifies the mechanism more precisely: the autonomic nervous system becomes chronically "tuned" beyond a threshold of intensity, failing to return to baseline — a dynamical system stuck in an attractor it cannot escape without external perturbation.

At the organizational scale, the same architecture appears in different clothing. Argyris found that virtually all participants in his studies operated from "Model I" theories-in-use — characterized by unilateral control, winning, and suppressing negative feelings — that create self-reinforcing defensive routines blocking double-loop learning. Organizations using Model I behavior generate "self-fulfilling prophecies, self-fueling processes, and escalating error." DiMaggio and Powell's institutional isomorphism explains why organizations adopt and maintain practices not because they work but because they are seen as legitimate — deviation risks loss of social standing, creating powerful inertia against genuine model revision. Irving Janis's groupthink research shows how cohesive groups develop an "illusion of unanimity" where self-censorship, direct pressure on dissenters, and collective rationalization systematically suppress the prediction errors that would force model updating.

At the civilizational scale, Nate Hagens frames the same pathology in thermodynamic terms. Industrial civilization operates under pathologically precise priors: **infinite growth is possible, resources are substitutable, the monetary economy accurately represents biophysical reality**. These priors have become refractory to the mounting prediction errors from ecological and energy systems. The superorganism uses debt — pulling resources from the future — as a mechanism to temporarily suppress these errors, analogous to how traumatized systems use avoidance to prevent encountering disconfirming evidence. Joseph Tainter adds the energetic dimension: as maintenance costs of existing complexity consume all available surplus, the system loses its capacity for adaptive response precisely when it needs it most. **Seven of nine planetary boundaries are now exceeded**, yet the civilizational model continues to predict approximately 3% annual growth into the indefinite future.

The common pattern is unmistakable: rigidity at every scale involves (1) priors that have become immune to disconfirmation, (2) mechanisms that attenuate or suppress prediction errors, (3) insufficient energy surplus for exploring alternatives, and (4) the absence of viable competing models.

## What enables updating: five cross-scale conditions

Five conditions recur across every discipline as necessary for successful model updating. Their specific implementations vary by scale, but their functional roles are isomorphic.

**First, optimal disequilibrium.** Every framework identifies a "Goldilocks zone" of disturbance — enough prediction error to destabilize rigid priors, not so much that the system fragments. Piaget called this disequilibrium; Heifetz calls it the "productive zone of disequilibrium"; Peter Levine calls it titration; in complexity science it corresponds to the "edge of chaos" where Stuart Kauffman and Chris Langton demonstrated that complex adaptive systems achieve maximal computational capability at the critical boundary between order and disorder. In the subcritical regime, the system is too rigid to process information effectively. In the supercritical regime, information is lost to noise. At criticality, the system balances sensitivity and stability — **precisely the balance needed for model updating**.

**Second, a holding environment.** Successful updating requires a container that provides simultaneous support and challenge. Kegan identifies three elements: confirmation (supporting where the person currently is), contradiction (challenging the limits of the current model), and continuity (maintaining relationship through the transition). Winnicott's psychoanalytic "holding environment," Heifetz's adaptive leadership framework, the therapeutic alliance in trauma therapy, and Turner's ritual structure during liminal periods all serve this identical function. Even at the ecological scale, Holling's "remember" function — where larger, slower systems constrain how smaller systems reorganize — provides structural holding during the adaptive cycle's backloop. The holding environment is what makes the difference between a prediction error that triggers updating and one that triggers defensive rigidity or collapse.

**Third, sufficient energy surplus.** Model updating is not free. It requires energy above what maintenance of existing structures demands. This is the two-layer dissipation framework: maintenance dissipation keeps the current system running; adaptive dissipation funds exploration and reorganization. Tainter demonstrated that societies facing diminishing returns on complexity investment progressively lose adaptive capacity as maintenance costs consume all surplus. At the neural level, neuromodulators — dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, norepinephrine — function as precision-gating signals that require metabolic resources to deploy. The REBUS model's "simulated annealing" metaphor is thermodynamically literal: heating a system to allow reorganization requires energy input. **A system that cannot afford the energetic cost of exploration is condemned to rigidity.**

**Fourth, available alternative models.** You cannot update to nothing. Lakatos demonstrated that scientists rationally cling to a degenerating research programme until a progressive rival appears. Wallace's revitalization movements require a prophetic figure who articulates a new "mazeway." Kuhn's paradigm shifts require an alternative framework that resolves the anomalies bedeviling the old one. At the developmental level, Kegan's subject-object shifts require a new epistemological vantage point from which the old one becomes visible. At the neural level, Gül Dölen's research on psychedelics reopening critical periods shows that plasticity without new experiential input produces nothing — the "iPlasticity" concept emphasizes that psychedelics create a *permissive* state that must be stabilized by new experience and context. Seeds of the new model must exist before the old one dissolves.

**Fifth, mechanisms for registering prediction errors rather than suppressing them.** This is the precision-weighting problem. Kegan and Lahey's "Immunity to Change" work surfaces the hidden competing commitments that actively work against desired change — an internal immune system that suppresses the prediction errors that would motivate transformation. Argyris's defensive routines serve the same function organizationally. Avoidance behaviors in PTSD prevent encounter with disconfirming evidence. At the civilizational scale, media ecosystems, political incentive structures, and economic indicators can either amplify or suppress signals of model failure. The practical lever is clear: **creating conditions where prediction errors are registered with appropriate precision rather than explained away**.

## How updating works across scales: the universal cycle

A remarkably consistent six-phase cycle appears across every discipline studied:

**Phase 1 — Steady state.** The system operates within its current generative model, solving problems through parameter adjustment (single-loop learning, normal science, assimilation, exploitation phase). Prediction errors are small and manageable. Energy is efficiently allocated between maintenance and modest adaptation.

**Phase 2 — Anomaly accumulation.** Prediction errors begin exceeding the model's capacity to absorb them. In science, anomalies resist explanation. In psychology, disequilibrium builds. In organizations, performance gaps widen. In ecology, slow variables drift toward thresholds. In civilizations, the gap between institutional predictions and lived reality grows. Critically, the system initially responds by increasing the precision of its existing priors — doubling down, explaining away, adding epicycles. Lakatos's "protective belt" of auxiliary hypotheses surrounds the hard core.

**Phase 3 — Crisis and dissolution.** The system reaches a tipping point where its existing model can no longer be maintained. Kuhn describes the loss of confidence in the paradigm; Turner describes separation from normal social structures; Holling describes the Ω release phase where tightly coupled structures cascade into failure; van der Kolk describes the moment when traumatic defenses become unsustainable. This phase is characterized by increased variance, critical slowing down, and "flickering" between states — all empirically documented as early warning signals of regime shifts by Marten Scheffer's group. The key insight from complexity science is that **the system's very optimization during the steady state creates the conditions for its crisis** — tight coupling, reduced redundancy, and depleted diversity make the K-phase system increasingly brittle.

**Phase 4 — Liminal exploration.** The dissolved system enters a state of heightened openness and uncertainty. Turner's liminality: "betwixt and between," stripped of previous identities. The REBUS model's flattened energy landscape. Holling's α phase of maximum uncertainty and maximum potential for innovation. Kuhn's "extraordinary science" where the proliferation of competing articulations and willingness to try anything characterizes the field. In adult development, Kegan's transitional space where the old subject-object structure has loosened but the new one hasn't crystallized. This phase is simultaneously the most creative and the most dangerous — it cannot be sustained indefinitely without structure or it degrades into chaos (Turner warned that "liminality is a state of great intensity that cannot exist very long without some sort of structure to stabilize it").

**Phase 5 — Model selection and crystallization.** A new generative model emerges and begins accumulating evidence. In science, a new paradigm resolves the old anomalies while opening productive new research directions. In Wallace's revitalization movements, a reformulated mazeway is communicated and organized around. In psychedelic therapy, the post-experience integration period stabilizes revised beliefs. In ecology, pioneer species colonize the reorganization phase and new structures begin self-reinforcing. The model selection process is not purely rational — Kuhn compared it to a Gestalt switch; Lakatos emphasized comparative problem-solving effectiveness; community consensus, available institutional support, and the model's "fit" with broader cultural narratives all matter.

**Phase 6 — Routinization and new steady state.** The new model is institutionalized, internalized, and becomes the taken-for-granted framework. Turner's incorporation phase. Kuhn's new normal science. The r-phase of the next adaptive cycle. The developmental consolidation of a new Kegan order. The therapeutic integration of new neural patterns stabilized by experience. Importantly, this phase sows the seeds of the next cycle — every new steady state will eventually encounter its own anomalies.

## Practical levers for facilitating model updating at each scale

**At the individual level**, the most powerful interventions work by modulating precision. Psychedelics — psilocybin, MDMA, LSD — pharmacologically relax the precision of high-level priors through 5-HT2A agonism, creating what Carhart-Harris calls a "flattened energy landscape" where rigid attractors loosen. Gül Dölen's 2023 Nature paper demonstrated that psychedelics literally reopen critical periods for social reward learning in adult mammals, with reopening duration proportional to subjective trip length. But the drug alone is insufficient: the iPlasticity framework emphasizes that new connections must be stabilized by experience and context during the window of enhanced plasticity. This is why psychedelic-assisted therapy combines pharmacological prior-relaxation with therapeutic integration — set and setting are not optional additions but structurally necessary.

Meditation operates through a different mechanism toward similar ends. Laukkonen and Slagter's comprehensive account describes meditation as progressively reducing the temporal depth of predictive processing — from focused attention (concentrating precision on one object) through open monitoring (releasing concentrated precision altogether) to non-dual awareness (deconstructing even the predicted self). Where psychedelics are pharmacological disruption, meditation is attentional deconstruction. Somatic approaches — Levine's Somatic Experiencing, Gendlin's Focusing, Feldenkrais — work by increasing interoceptive precision, bringing awareness to body-level prediction errors where rigid models are stored. The "felt sense" in Gendlin's framework is essentially an unresolved interoceptive prediction error carrying implicit information about what needs to change.

**At the organizational level**, Senge's five disciplines provide the most comprehensive toolkit: personal mastery (individual model-updating capacity), mental models work (explicitly surfacing and testing assumptions through the "ladder of inference"), shared vision (generating genuine commitment to a new model rather than mere compliance), team learning (Bohm's dialogue — suspending assumptions to enable collective model revision), and systems thinking (seeing feedback loops and leverage points rather than linear causation). Heifetz's adaptive leadership adds the crucial distinction between technical challenges (solvable within the existing model) and adaptive challenges (requiring model revision itself) — "the single most common source of leadership failure is treating adaptive challenges as technical problems." His practical framework — get on the balcony, regulate distress, protect voices from below, give the work back — creates the organizational equivalent of a therapeutic holding environment.

Kegan and Lahey's Immunity to Change process deserves special attention as a bridge between individual and organizational scales. Their four-column mapping surfaces hidden competing commitments and "big assumptions" that constitute an internal immune system actively resisting desired change. By designing safe-to-fail experiments to test these assumptions, the process converts implicit priors into explicit hypotheses — making what was subject into object, enabling revision. Their concept of "Deliberately Developmental Organizations" — workplaces that make adult development part of core strategy — represents the institutional equivalent of a contemplative practice: continuous, structured model-updating as an organizational norm.

**At the civilizational scale**, the levers are less well-tested but can be inferred from the cross-scale isomorphism. Increasing **modularity** — enabling regions, communities, and subsystems to experiment independently without cascading destabilization — corresponds to Holling's panarchy insight that nested adaptive cycles allow subsystem reorganization without consuming the whole. Preserving **diversity** — of institutions, practices, energy systems, food systems, knowledge traditions — provides the raw material for recombination during reorganization, analogous to the ecological principle that response diversity within functional groups enhances resilience. Maintaining **weak ties** between otherwise isolated communities enables the cross-pollination of novel ideas, following Granovetter's finding that weak ties serve as crucial bridges for non-redundant information flow. And creating **legitimate spaces for heterodox thinking** — institutional equivalents of Kuhn's "extraordinary science" — allows alternative generative models to develop before the crisis that will demand them.

## Crisis versus gradual learning: a false dichotomy resolved

The evidence resolves an apparent paradox. Crisis appears as a near-universal precondition for paradigm-level model updating across every domain studied. Kuhn requires anomaly-driven crisis before paradigm shifts. Wallace's revitalization movements require cultural distortion before reformulation. The Meiji Restoration required Perry's Black Ships. Post-WWII German and Japanese reconstruction required total defeat. The Axial Age may have been catalyzed by the Bronze Age Collapse. Holling's adaptive cycle requires release (Ω) before reorganization (α). Yet gradual learning — Lakatos's progressive research programmes, Laudan's evolving research traditions, meditation practice, long-term therapy, institutional learning — clearly produces real model updating without catastrophe.

The resolution lies in the hierarchical structure of generative models. **Gradual learning effectively updates lower- and mid-level predictions** — the parameters and contingencies within an existing model framework. This corresponds to single-loop learning, normal science, assimilation, and exploitation-phase optimization. **Crisis is required primarily for updating the highest-level priors** — the paradigmatic assumptions, deep identity structures, and civilizational operating systems that constrain interpretation at all levels below. High-level priors resist updating because they have accumulated enormous model evidence over long periods, because they constrain the interpretation of lower-level signals (prediction errors that would challenge them are "explained away" at lower levels), and because revising them cascades changes throughout the entire hierarchy at enormous energetic cost.

The optimal strategy, visible across every successful case, is what might be called **structured disruption**: using controlled perturbation to open windows of plasticity, combined with guided experience to stabilize adaptive new patterns. This is the logic of psychedelic-assisted therapy (pharmacological prior-relaxation plus therapeutic integration), Heifetz's productive zone of disequilibrium (enough pressure to motivate change without overwhelming), Levine's titration (gradual, dosed exposure to activation), and the ritual structure of rites of passage (separation, liminal exploration, incorporation). The Meiji Restoration exemplifies this at civilizational scale: the Perry crisis opened the window; the reformers provided structured direction; selective integration of foreign models with Japanese cultural identity stabilized the new configuration.

**Destructive crisis**, by contrast, occurs when dissolution exceeds the system's capacity for structured reorganization — when institutional capacity collapses alongside ideological crisis, when no viable alternative model exists, when the liminal period becomes permanent anomie, or when the crisis is captured by regressive movements that impose rigid, fear-based models rather than enabling genuine updating. The difference between constructive and destructive crisis maps precisely onto whether the five enabling conditions — optimal disequilibrium, holding environment, energy surplus, available alternatives, and error-registration mechanisms — are present or absent.

## Historical proof of concept: when civilizations actually updated

The historical record confirms that large-scale model updating is possible but rare, and it follows the patterns predicted by the cross-disciplinary framework.

**The Meiji Restoration (1868–1912)** stands as perhaps the most dramatic example of deliberate civilizational model-updating. Perry's Black Ships created the existential prediction error; a coalition of reformist samurai provided the alternative model by selectively importing Prussian constitutional structures, German military organization, British naval architecture, and American educational philosophy; the restored emperor provided the holding environment of cultural continuity through radical institutional change. Within 44 years, Japan transformed from a feudal agricultural society to a power that defeated Russia in 1905. The key insight: successful large-scale updating requires **selective integration rather than wholesale replacement** — revising the generative model while preserving core identity structures that provide continuity through transformation.

**Post-WWII Germany and Japan** demonstrate how catastrophic defeat can function as a forced liminal period. The utter discrediting of prior political-military leadership created what Turner would recognize as the separation phase; Allied occupation provided external holding and alternative institutional templates; pre-existing institutional capabilities (education infrastructure, bureaucratic expertise) provided the seeds for reconstruction. Germany's *Vergangenheitsbewältigung* — coming to terms with the Nazi past — required decades and generational turnover, supporting Planck's principle that model updating at the collective level partly depends on the replacement of carriers of the old model by those socialized into the new one. A 2019 study in the *American Economic Review* by Azoulay and colleagues confirmed empirically that after eminent scientists die, non-collaborators enter the field bringing newer ideas — **the prospect of challenging a luminary serves as a deterrent for entry by outsiders**.

**The Axial Age (800–200 BCE)** — when China, India, Persia, Judea, and Greece simultaneously developed new philosophical frameworks — may represent the most consequential civilizational model updating in human history. Jaspers described it as the moment when "hitherto unconsciously accepted ideas, customs and conditions were subjected to examination, questioned and liquidated." It emerged in contexts of political fragmentation and competing small states, which created intellectual ferment and freedom for novel thought — precisely the conditions of modularity and diversity that complexity science identifies as prerequisites for adaptive reorganization. Some scholars hypothesize the preceding Bronze Age Collapse created the civilizational liminal period that enabled these breakthroughs.

## Conclusion: what this map reveals about our current moment

The cross-disciplinary convergence documented here is not merely intellectually satisfying — it has urgent practical implications. If Hagens is right that industrial civilization is running an increasingly miscalibrated generative model, and if Tainter is right that maintenance costs of existing complexity are consuming the energy surplus needed for adaptation, then the framework identifies both the danger and the path.

The danger is clear: a system that has lost its capacity for epistemic foraging, that suppresses prediction errors through debt and denial, that has optimized away the modularity, diversity, and redundancy needed for reorganization, and that faces declining energy surplus precisely when adaptive investment is most needed — this system is approaching what complexity science calls a fold bifurcation, where the current attractor simply ceases to exist.

But the path is equally clear. **Every tradition studied points to the same set of interventions, adapted to scale**: loosen rigid priors (through contemplative practice, psychedelic experience, deliberate exposure to disconfirming evidence, or honest accounting of biophysical reality); create holding environments that can contain the anxiety of model revision (therapeutic relationships, adaptive leadership, institutional spaces for heterodox thinking, community resilience structures); preserve and cultivate diversity of approaches, institutions, and knowledge systems (the seeds from which new models crystallize); build modularity that allows subsystem experimentation without cascading failure; and — perhaps most importantly — ensure that the system retains sufficient energy surplus and organizational slack to invest in exploration rather than spending everything on maintaining structures that no longer serve.

The deepest insight may be Kegan's subject-object shift applied to civilization itself: the generative model that currently operates *as* our civilizational identity — growth, progress, technological substitution — must become something we *have* rather than something we *are*. Only when the model becomes visible as a model, rather than being mistaken for reality itself, does genuine revision become possible. This is what advanced meditators describe, what psychedelics catalyze, what developmental maturity enables, and what the current historical moment demands. The question is whether the loosening can be structured — held within a container that enables creative reorganization — or whether it will arrive as unstructured collapse. The research suggests this is not predetermined. But the window for structured transition narrows as maintenance costs rise, energy surplus shrinks, and the gap between the model and reality continues to widen.
